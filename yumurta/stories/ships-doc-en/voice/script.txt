April twenty-third, fourteen fifty-three. Dawn. The defenders of Constantinople look down at the Golden Horn, the one harbor they were sure was safe… and it's full of enemy warships. Dozens of them. The giant chain guarding the entrance? Still there. Unbroken. Nobody sailed past it. So how did the ships get in? They sailed over a hill. This is how a twenty-one-year-old sultan pulled off one of the boldest moves in military history.

To understand why this was such a shock, you need to know what Mehmed the Second was up against. Constantinople had stood for over a thousand years, the last capital of the Roman Empire in the east. Water on two sides, and on the third, the most famous walls of the Middle Ages: a moat, an outer wall, and a massive inner wall, with nearly two hundred towers. No army had ever smashed through them. Mehmed brought around eighty thousand men, and a monster cannon built by a Hungarian engineer named Orban, firing stone balls that weighed more than half a ton. Inside, Emperor Constantine the Eleventh had fewer than eight thousand defenders.

But the city had a weak spot, and everyone knew it. Back in twelve oh-four, crusaders had taken Constantinople from the Golden Horn, where the sea walls were low. So this time, the Byzantines stretched a massive chain across the mouth of the harbor, floating on wooden logs, from the city to the tower of Galata. Behind it waited their ships. As long as the chain held, the harbor was safe, and every soldier could be sent to the land walls.

And it held. Mehmed's fleet attacked it, and failed. Then, on April twentieth, four Christian supply ships fought their way straight through the entire Ottoman navy, while the whole city cheered from the walls. Mehmed was so furious that he rode his horse into the sea, shouting at his admiral. The chain was lowered, the four ships slipped inside, and the chain went back up. Two weeks into the siege, the young sultan was losing.

So Mehmed made a decision that sounded insane. If his ships couldn't go through the chain… they would go around it. Over land. Behind the Genoese town of Galata rose a hill, and on the other side lay the Golden Horn, safely behind the chain. His men cleared a road, laid down wooden tracks and rollers, and greased them with animal fat. Dragging ships overland wasn't a brand-new idea. But over a hill, past a neutral town, in a single night? Nobody saw it coming.

On the night of April twenty-second, the work began. One by one, ships were pulled out of the Bosphorus onto wooden cradles. Teams of oxen and thousands of men hauled them up the slope, the hulls creaking over the greased rollers. And here's the strangest part. According to the chroniclers, the sailors sat at their oars, rowing through the air, sails raised, as if they were already at sea, while drums and trumpets played. Ship after ship climbed the hill, and slid down the other side. By morning, around seventy ships were floating in the Golden Horn.

The chain was never broken. It just didn't matter anymore. Now the defenders had to guard the harbor walls too, pulling men away from the land walls, where they were desperately needed. A Venetian night raid to burn the new fleet was betrayed, and failed. Then the Ottomans built a floating bridge across the harbor. On May twenty-ninth, fourteen fifty-three, came the final assault. The defenders held for hours, until their best commander, Giovanni Giustiniani, was wounded and carried away. The lines broke. The last emperor died fighting at the walls, and more than a thousand years of Roman history came to an end. The ships on the hill didn't win the siege alone. But in a single night, they turned a wall of water into just another front.
