On the morning of April twenty-third, fourteen fifty-three, the defenders of Constantinople looked down at the one stretch of water they thought was perfectly safe… and saw enemy warships floating in it. Dozens of them. The harbor was still locked. The great chain was still in place. Nobody had broken through. So how did the ships get in? They came over a hill.

In fourteen fifty-three, Constantinople was more than a city. For over a thousand years it had been the capital of the Eastern Roman Empire, the Byzantine Empire. It sat on a triangle of land, with water on two sides, and the strongest walls of the medieval world on the third. The land walls, built in the fifth century, ran for about six kilometers: a moat, an outer wall, and a towering inner wall, with nearly two hundred towers. For a thousand years, no army had ever broken through them. But by fourteen fifty-three, the empire around the city was almost gone. What was left was a proud, half-empty capital, surrounded by the rising power of the Ottomans.

The Ottoman sultan was Mehmed the Second. He was just twenty-one years old, and he had one goal: to take the city that his ancestors had failed to take. He prepared for it like an engineer. He built a fortress on the Bosphorus to cut off help from the north. He hired a Hungarian cannon-maker named Orban. Orban had first offered his services to the Byzantine emperor, but the emperor couldn't afford him. Mehmed paid him four times what he asked. Orban cast a bronze bombard so huge it needed dozens of oxen to pull it, and its stone balls weighed more than half a ton. In early April, he arrived outside the walls with an army of perhaps eighty thousand men. Inside, the last emperor, Constantine the Eleventh, had fewer than eight thousand defenders, among them a band of Genoese soldiers led by a famous captain, Giovanni Giustiniani.

The defenders knew where the weak point was. In twelve oh-four, crusaders had captured Constantinople not from the land, but from the harbor, the Golden Horn, where the sea walls were lower and weaker. So before the Ottomans arrived, the Byzantines stretched an enormous chain across the mouth of the Golden Horn, from the city to the tower of Galata on the other side. It floated on wooden logs, and behind it waited the Christian ships. As long as the chain held, the harbor walls didn't need many defenders. And every soldier saved there could fight on the land walls.

Mehmed's cannons pounded the land walls day after day, but every night the defenders rebuilt the damage. His fleet attacked the chain, and failed. Then, on April twentieth, four Christian ships carrying supplies sailed straight through the Ottoman navy, in front of the whole city. The sultan was so furious that he rode his horse into the sea, shouting orders at his admiral. The four ships reached the chain, it was lowered for them, and they slipped into the harbor. The siege was two weeks old, and it was going badly.

So Mehmed decided that if his ships couldn't go through the chain, they would go around it. Over land. Behind the Genoese colony of Galata rose a hill. The Genoese there were officially neutral, trading with both sides, and the Ottomans would pass right outside their walls. On the other side was the Golden Horn, behind the chain. His men cleared a road over it, laid down wooden rails and rollers, and covered them with fat and oil. The idea wasn't completely new. Only fourteen years earlier, the Venetians had hauled a fleet over the mountains to Lake Garda in Italy. But no one expected it here, and certainly not this fast.

On the night of April twenty-second, the operation began. Ships were pulled from the Bosphorus onto cradles, and teams of oxen and men hauled them up the slope on the greased rollers. According to the chroniclers, the sailors sat at their oars and the sails were raised, as if the ships were already at sea, while drums and trumpets played. By morning, around seventy ships had crossed the hill and slid down into the Golden Horn. These weren't giant warships. Most were light galleys and smaller vessels. But they were enough.

The chain had not been broken, but it no longer mattered. Now the defenders had to guard the long sea walls along the harbor, and pull men away from the land walls, where they were desperately needed. On April twenty-eighth, the Venetians tried to burn the new Ottoman fleet in a night raid. The Ottomans had been warned. The attack failed, and the lead ship was sunk. Soon the Ottomans built a floating bridge across the Golden Horn, letting troops and cannons reach the harbor walls. The city was now under pressure from almost every side.

Before dawn on May twenty-ninth, fourteen fifty-three, after nearly two months of siege, the Ottomans launched their final assault. For hours the defenders held. Then Giustiniani was badly wounded and carried from the walls. According to one famous story, a small gate called the Kerkoporta had been left open, and Ottoman soldiers slipped through. The defense broke, and the last Byzantine emperor died fighting at the walls. Constantinople fell. More than a thousand years of Roman rule in the east came to an end, and the city became the new Ottoman capital: Istanbul. The ships that crossed the hill didn't win the siege by themselves. But in a single night, they turned an impossible wall of water into just another front, and showed what a twenty-one-year-old sultan was willing to try.
