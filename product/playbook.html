Who this is for

You want to run a faceless animated Shorts channel (Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts, TikTok) that looks like a real studio made it, without drawing or knowing how to animate.

You don't need to know how to code. In this workflow an AI agent writes and runs all the code. Your job is to choose the story, give directions and say what looks wrong. You do need to be comfortable copying a few commands and reading what the AI tells you.

An honest note before we start

  • This is not a "one click, viral video" button. A Short takes me a few hours of AI work and about 30–60 minutes of my own attention: picking the story, checking pictures and asking for fixes.
  • Views come from the story first. A great-looking video with a weak hook still flops. That's why a big part of this guide is about scripts.
  • AI tools change fast. Model names, quotas and prices in this guide are correct at the time of writing; check the current ones before you start.
01

The basics

What happens between "I have an idea" and "it's posted," and which tool does each part.

The big picture

How a Just Needy Short is made

Every video goes through the same ten steps. An AI agent does the heavy lifting in each one; you approve and correct.

1TopicA true, surprising story or a mind-bending question.
2Script~120–200 words in a proven structure.
3VoiceAI narrator reads it with director's notes.
4TimingEvery spoken word gets a timestamp.
5Scenes3D sets and egg-head characters, built in code.
6CaptionsBig bold 3–4-word captions, timed to the voice.
7SoundQuiet music and a few sound effects, pinned to words.
8RenderCheck stills first, then render 1080×1920.
9Final checkLoudness, length, a last look at the frames.
10PostSame time every day, one clear call to action.

The one idea that makes it all work: everything is pinned to words

Most AI video tools generate random clips and you cut them together by hand. This workflow works the other way around. The voice comes first. Every word in it gets a timestamp, and every picture, camera move, caption and sound effect is attached to a word, not to a second.

Say the narrator says "...and the head rolled onto the floor." The head starts falling exactly on "rolled" and the thud lands on "floor." If you change the voice later, everything moves with it automatically. That is why the videos feel edited by a professional even though nobody touched a timeline.

03

Making a video,
step by step

The full process for one Short, in the order I do it, with what you say to Claude at each step and what to check.

Step 1

Pick a topic that already wants to be watched

The topic is 80% of the result. A strong topic has a question the viewer must hear answered.

What works on my channel

  • Hidden mechanisms: someone does something that looks absurd, and the reason only comes at the end (escaping Alcatraz with spoons, painting a jail cell pink).
  • Big what-ifs: a huge number made visible (splitting the Earth like a cake between 8 billion people).
  • Paradoxes: a question with no answer that everyone has an opinion on.
  • Relatable comedy: countdowns and deadpan tutorials people send to a friend.

Where to find topics

Fact-based
  • Wikipedia: "List of prison escapes," "List of paradoxes," "List of cognitive biases," "Unusual deaths," "Hoaxes"
  • History stories with a clever trick inside
  • Science "why does…" questions
Trend-based
  • Shorts that blew up in another language: the topic and format usually haven't been done in English yet
  • Your own comments: "do one about ___"
  • The sequel to your best video
Take the idea, not the words. Facts and formats can't be owned. "Split the Earth between 8 billion people" is a fact-based idea anyone can make. Someone else's script, their exact sentences, jokes and order, belongs to them, and translating it word for word is still copying. When a video in another language inspires you, ask Claude to research the facts from scratch and write a new script in your own structure. Your video will be better anyway, because it's built for your audience.

Score it before you make it

QuestionScore
Can I say the hook in one sentence a 10-year-old understands?0–2
Is there something absurd or surprising I can SHOW in the first 2 seconds?0–2
Is there an answer the viewer has to wait for?0–2
Will people argue about it or send it to someone?0–2
Are the facts checkable in two reliable sources?0–2

8 or more: make it. 5–7: find a stronger hook. Under 5: skip it.

Step 2

Write the script

I give Claude a title and one line. Claude researches the facts, writes the script in one of the five structures from Part 4, and lists its sources. I read it out loud and ask for changes.

# What I actually type
New video: "They Painted a Prison PINK to Stop Fights?!"
One line: in 1979 a scientist said pink calms angry men, so a Navy jail
painted a cell pink.
Use the hidden-mechanism structure. Check every fact in 2 sources and list
them. ~200 words. Show me the script before doing anything else.

The rules every script follows

  • First sentence = the hook. It shows the strangest moment of the story without explaining it.
  • Short sentences. About 3.4 seconds each on average. If you need a breath in the middle, split it.
  • Pace: about 3–3.7 English words per second once sped up. 200 words ≈ 60–65 s.
  • Everything planted pays off. If you mention the accordion early, it has to come back (it becomes the pump).
  • A turn in the middle. The laughing stops; the plan works; the "fact" turns out to be wrong.
  • Show the proof before the explanation. The small version of the payoff first, then the big one.
  • End within ~4 seconds of the reveal. No long goodbye.

Fact-checking is not optional

On a fact channel, one wrong number in the comments can bury a video. My rule for Claude: check every fact in two sources; if they disagree on a number, don't say the number ("hundreds of," not "275"). Round numbers and say "about."

Step 3

Give it a voice

One AI narrator for every video. A consistent voice is part of your brand, just like the characters.

The setup I use

ModelGemini TTS (flash TTS model, through Google AI Studio). Keep a fallback model in mind for days the free quota runs out.
VoicePuck (prebuilt). Young, bright, clear: a good fit for curious and funny stories.
RecordingThe whole script in ONE request, so tone and pace stay consistent. Lines separated by blank lines.
Save every takeCache each recording in the repo, so re-rendering never spends quota again.
After recordingTrim long pauses (sentence end ~0.22 s, comma ~0.14 s), then speed up ×1.12.

Director's notes: the secret to a voice that doesn't sound robotic

Gemini TTS follows written directions. Put them above the transcript: one base note that never changes, and a few line-level notes for the moments that matter.

### DIRECTOR'S NOTES
Narrator of a witty YouTube Shorts / Instagram Reels animation channel.
Voice: young adult American male, energetic but natural, clean and bright,
medium-fast pace, lightly sarcastic and clever. Natural pauses and comic
timing. Not deep, not booming, not a documentary voice, never shouting,
no fake excitement.
The first line is the hook: punchy and intriguing.
'The guards laugh.' amused; '"Pink? For these guys?"' mocking.
'Not the paint. Time.' slow, a small reveal.
Leave short pauses between lines (about a third of a second).
Speak only the transcript below, nothing else.

#### TRANSCRIPT
This man tried to calm down violent prisoners...

The API call itself is small. You won't need to write it, Claude does, but this is what happens under the hood:

# POST to generativelanguage.googleapis.com/v1beta/models/<tts-model>:generateContent
{ "contents": [{ "parts": [{ "text": "<director's notes + transcript>" }] }],
  "generationConfig": {
    "responseModalities": ["AUDIO"],
    "speechConfig": { "voiceConfig": { "prebuiltVoiceConfig": { "voiceName": "Puck" } } } } }
# the answer is 24 kHz mono audio; save it as a .wav
Step 4

Give every word a timestamp

This is the step that makes the whole video feel professionally edited.

Whisper listens to the narration and returns when each word starts and ends. Whisper sometimes mishears names and numbers ("Alan" for "Allen," "50" for "fifty"). So the tool keeps your script's spelling and only borrows Whisper's times, matching the two word lists side by side.

{"words": [
  {"word": "This",   "start": 0.03, "end": 0.18},
  {"word": "man",    "start": 0.18, "end": 0.39},
  {"word": "tried",  "start": 0.39, "end": 0.61}, ... ]}

Then two small extras come from the same audio:

  • Mouth envelope: how loud the voice is at each moment, so a talking character's mouth flaps in sync.
  • Pause trimming: long gaps cut to fixed lengths before speeding up (sentence end .22 s, comma .14 s, inside a phrase .09 s).

How the scene uses it

In the scene file, nothing is typed in seconds. Everything points at a word:

// camera whips to the jail on the word "Navy"; the guards laugh on "laugh"
{ t: W('Navy') - .15, move: 'whip', pos: [...], look: [...] }
guard.draw({ expr: t > W('laugh') ? 'laugh' : 'neutral' })

W('word', 2) means the 2nd time that word is said. Re-record the voice, re-run the timing, and every action lands on its new word automatically.

Step 5

Build the scenes

This is where the engine and Claude do the work. You describe what each moment should show; Claude writes the sets, characters, props and camera in a single story file.

What a scene is made of

Sets3D rooms and places (a jail cell, a beach, a lab), placed far apart in one 3D world. The camera flies or whips between them.
CharactersFlat white egg-head cartoon cards standing in the 3D world, with ten expressions (neutral, smile, smug, focused, angry, proud, surprised, worried, laugh, grin), blinking, mouth flaps and real shadows.
Props3D objects (buckets, spoons, lamps) or painted textures (tiles, posters).
CameraA list of key moments, each pinned to a word: glide, whip (fast pan with motion blur) or cut.
LookPer-shot focus, blur, colour, glow and vignette.

The style rules that make it look expensive

These come from measuring popular egg-head Shorts frame by frame (they're written up in the engine's style book). Keep them in CLAUDE.md:

  • The hook is 3–4 pictures in the first 5 seconds, one per phrase of the first sentence. In frame one the character is already doing the strange thing.
  • The camera never stands still. Holds slowly push in; at most one or two hard cuts per video.
  • Every set has ONE light you can see: a lamp, a window, the moon. Warm against cool, with real shadows. Flat light is the #1 reason a render looks cheap.
  • No pure black. The darkest colour is navy or plum. Warm and cool colours share every frame.
  • Heads stay white in every scene; only a hint of the room's light touches them.
  • Characters who aren't acting stand still, with no idle bobbing. They blink every 1–2.5 seconds, and their expression changes with the beat.
  • Hands really touch things. If a character holds a spoon, the hand is on the spoon in every frame.
  • Big walls need a pattern (tiles, a painted band, a poster), or the frame looks empty.

How I direct this step

Now build the video. Copy my newest finished story as the starting point.
Scenes: (1) hook: the scientist with a bucket, splashing pink on a cell wall,
(2) 1979 lab with a pink card, (3) Navy jail, guards laughing, ...

"bucket", "1979", "laugh", "California", "Time", "pink" (last).
Show me a contact sheet before the full render.
Step 6

Captions

Most people start watching on mute. Captions are not optional, and the style of your captions is part of your brand.

FontPoppins ExtraBold (free, Google Fonts). I picked it from samples, so pick yours once and never change it.
Size and outlineBig (about 60 px on a 1080-wide frame), white with a dark outline and a soft shadow.
Chunk3–4 words per caption (max ~22 characters), appearing exactly with the words.
PositionAbout 70% down the screen. Faces live at 35–60% height, so captions never cover them. Keep clear of the bottom, where the app's buttons are.
NumbersWrite digits on screen ("2.5 million") even when the voice says them in words.

The engine's caption settings, in one line:

CAPTION = { font: '"Poppins", "Arial Black", sans-serif', size: 60, weight: 800,
            stroke: .13, shadow: .25, y: .7, mode: 'phrase', maxChars: 22 }
Also export an .srt file (subtitles) for YouTube. It helps accessibility and search.
Step 7

Sound

Good sound is felt, not noticed. My biggest lesson: less is more.

  • Music: about 14 dB under the voice, ducking a bit further whenever the narrator talks. Change the mood with the story: suspense under the hook, playful while the idea spreads, suspense again at the turn, warm at the reveal.
  • Sound effects: sparse and soft, about one every 3–4 seconds, only on key words (a whoosh on a camera whip, a click, a splash). Every effect is pinned to a word, like everything else.
  • Opening bell: a very short, quiet "ding" at 0 seconds. A long ringing bell sounds cheap.
  • Avoid: crowd murmur beds, dramatic "dun" hits on every line, effects louder than the voice. A crowded soundtrack is tiring to listen to.

The music and effects are made from code (synthesized), so there are no copyright claims and no stock library to pay for.

Step 8

Check stills, then render

A full render takes time. A still frame takes seconds. Always look before you render.

  1. StillsAsk Claude for half-size stills at one moment per scene and per beat, laid out on one contact sheet. It takes minutes.
  2. LookGo through the list below. Ask for fixes in plain words: "the hat is cut off," "the room is too orange," "his hand isn't on the bucket."
  3. DraftRender the whole video at half size and watch it once from start to finish. Some problems only show in motion.
  4. FinalFull 1080×1920 at 30 fps. On the cloud, use the resumable render and let it run.

What to look for in the stills

  • Captions covering a face
  • A character cut by the edge of the frame
  • A hat or prop sliced off in a straight line
  • A hand in one place, its object somewhere else
  • A room with no visible light source
  • A room that's one colour (all orange)
  • Flat colour behind a window
  • Empty or dark frames during a camera whip
  • A character drifting out of frame at the END of a shot
  • Something blurry or flickering (watch 10 frames in a row)
  • The hook frame: is the strange thing visible at 0 s?
  • The last frame: does it pay off the story?
Fix only what changed. If one scene is wrong after a full render, ask Claude to delete just those frames and re-render them. The rest stays.

Before a video leaves the computer, it passes the same checks every time. Ask Claude to run them and show you the results.

Step 10

Post it

  • Same time every day. My audience is mostly in the US, so I post on weekdays at 12:00–14:00 US Eastern and on weekends a bit earlier. Find your own audience's time in Instagram's "Audience" tab.
  • One a day. Go to two a day only if your retention stays high (above ~70%).
  • Title: the hook as a question, plus one emoji. "They Painted a Prison PINK to Stop Fights?! 🩷"
  • Cover: a frame from the hook with a 2–4-word title in your caption font.
  • One call to action: paradox → "comment yes or no," what-if → "what should we split next?", comedy → "send this to…". Only one.
  • Turn viewers into followers: on my account 99.9% of views came from non-followers. A reason to follow ("Part 2 tomorrow," "a new paradox every day") matters as much as the video.

Read your numbers after 48 hours

If……then
Most people swipe in the first 3 sThe hook frame isn't strange enough. Show the act, not the setup.
People leave in the middleA beat repeats itself, or the turn comes too late.
High views, few followsAdd a series promise and make the next part.
One video beats the others by 10×Make a sequel in the same format this week.

Script formats

Five structures I use, each with the beats, a full example script written for this guide, a fill-in template and topic ideas.

Format 1

The Hidden Mechanism

My strongest story format. Someone does something absurd, everyone laughs, and the reason, a real piece of physics, chemistry or psychology, is only revealed at the very end. 60–70 s, ~200 words.

  1. Absurd act · 0 sShow the strange act. Don't explain it. "This prisoner is sculpting his own head out of soap."
  2. You see"You see, it's [year/place]." The context in two sentences.
  3. Plan + plantsThe steps, each with an object or detail that will come back later.
  4. DoubtSomeone laughs or objects, in a quoted line.
  5. Turn · ~50%The laughing stops, or the plan hits a wall.
  6. Small proofA small version of the payoff, shown before it's explained.
  7. Reveal · ~89%The mechanism in one short sentence.
  8. ButtonOne last image or line that pays off a plant. End within ~4 s.

Full example: "The Pink Prison" in Step 2. A second one of mine, "Alcatraz," follows the same beats: the soap head (hook), spoons, the accordion that covers the noise and later becomes the raft pump, the man left behind, the guard who reaches in and the head that rolls onto the floor.

Template

This [person] is [absurd act]... [with an odd object]. You see, it's [year], [place]. [Why it matters: one line]. [Step 1 of the plan, with a detail you'll reuse later]. [Problem?] [Clever fix, also reused later]. [Someone] laughs. "[Mocking line]" [Escalation: it works, or it spreads]. Then [the turn]... [Small proof: a little version of the result]. So what [really happened / made it work]? [The mechanism in one short sentence.] [Button: a last image that pays off an early detail.]

Format 2

The Big What-If

Nobody can picture 8 billion of anything. Turn a huge number into an everyday object and show the result. The viewer stays for the answer. 40–60 s.

  1. What ifOne question with a number answer. The thumbnail asks it too.
  2. Make it an objectThe Earth as a cake, the ocean as a bathtub, history as one day.
  3. Answer 1The first result, said plainly. Pause.
  4. Make it realCompare it to something the viewer knows: a football field, an island.
  5. Answers 2–4Each answer more surprising than the last. Save the craziest for last.
  6. RecapAll the numbers in one breath.
  7. CTA"What should we [split / try] next?" Every comment is a future topic.

Example script (written for this guide)

What ifWhat if all 8 billion people on Earth stood in one place?Number question, instantly clear.
Let's pack them in.Invites the viewer into the experiment.
Answer 1One square meter each. Room to breathe. / That's 8,000 square kilometers. / A bit smaller than Puerto Rico.Number, then a place the viewer can picture.
The whole human race... on one island.Pause. Let it land.
Answer 2Now squeeze. Concert crowd. Four people per square meter. / We fit in 2,000 square kilometers. / A city a bit bigger than London.Smaller and stranger.
TwistAnd if all of us jumped at the same time? / The Earth wouldn't even notice.The surprise: humans are tiny.
CTASo where should we put everyone? Tell me in the comments.An open question = comments.

Rounded figures from a world population of ~8 billion. As with every fact video, check the numbers again before you post.

What if [huge thing] were [split / packed / stacked] [between 8 billion people]? Let's [split it / try it]. [First answer]. That's about [everyday comparison]. [Second answer, more surprising]. But the real surprise? [Third answer]. [Recap: number, number, number...] What should we [split] next?

Format 3

The Paradox

Take something everyone thinks is obvious and show them it isn't. Calm and curious, like telling a friend a strange thought late at night. 45–60 s.

  1. Obvious2–3 facts nobody would argue with, in a quick rhythm.
  2. Crack"But here's something no one can prove." Lower the voice.
  3. QuestionThe paradox as one simple question.
  4. Title"This is the ___ paradox." A name is easy to remember and search.
  5. Explain3–5 short lines built around one everyday image.
  6. Twist"Here's the twist." It's bigger than it looked.
  7. Warm landingEnd on something comforting. Dark endings get fewer shares.
  8. CTAA yes/no or "which one are you" question.

Example script (written for this guide)

ObviousYour skin, your blood, your bones. All you, right?Three things, no argument.
CrackBut many of the cells in your body today weren't there ten years ago.The hook.
QuestionSo are you still the same person?Simple, personal.
TitleThis is the Ship of Theseus paradox.Named.
ExplainA hero's wooden ship sits in a harbor. / Every year one rotten plank is replaced. / After enough years... not one original plank is left. / Is it still his ship?One image, step by step.
Now, someone collects all the old planks... and builds them back into a ship. / Which one is the real one?Escalation.
TwistHere's the twist. Your body does this all the time. Cells are replaced, day after day.Brings it home (keep the claim general: not every cell is replaced).
LandingMaybe "you" isn't the planks. Maybe it's the shape they keep.Warm.
CTASame ship, or new ship? Comment your answer.One-word answer.

Template

[Fact 1], [fact 2], [fact 3]. [Agree?] But here's something no one can prove. What if [the paradox as one question]? This is the [name] paradox. [Everyday image that sets it up: 3–5 short lines.] Here's the twist. [It's bigger than it looked.] [Warm landing.] [Yes/no question]? Comment [answer].

Format 4

The Countdown

"Being ___ has 3 secret advantages." Fast, cheeky and made to be sent to a friend. 25–35 s.

  1. Hook"Being [group] has 3 secret advantages." Pick something people get teased about.
  2. Number + title"One: the back seat." Two or three words, instantly pictured.
  3. Others sufferShow the "normal" people struggling.
  4. Flip + escalateThe group wins, then push it one step too far. That's the laugh.
  5. ×3Same rhythm. The third is the most absurd.
  6. Send CTA"Send this to your [youngest] friend."

Example script (written for this guide)

HookBeing the youngest sibling has 3 secret advantages.A familiar tease, flipped.

#2Two: the mistakes. / The oldest walks into every trap first. / The youngest takes notes.Short and smug.
#3Three: the blame. / Something breaks. Everyone turns around. / The youngest is already crying. / Case closed.Most absurd last, like a courtroom drama.
CTASend this to your little brother or sister.Asks for a send.

Format 5

The Deadpan Tutorial

A tutorial for an embarrassingly simple task, read with a straight face. The whole joke is one step that's a little too true. 15–20 s, so it loops.

  1. Title"How to [simple task]." No joke yet.
  2. Steps 1–4Literal, obvious steps, read flat. A short pause after each "Step ___:".
  3. The stepOne step that calls out the real bad habit. Slower, pointed.
  4. MedalOver-the-top sincere congratulations.

MedalCongratulations. You have left the party.Proud, like a medal ceremony.

12 mistakes I made (so you don't have to)

MistakeWhat fixed it
Too many sound effectsOne effect every 3–4 seconds, soft, only on key words.
A long ringing bell at the startA very short, quiet ding. Long bells sound cheap.
Crowd murmur under scenesRemoved. It made the mix muddy and tiring.
A slow opening (character just standing)The strange act is on screen in frame one: 3–4 pictures in the first 5 s.
Flat, even lightingOne visible light per set, warm vs cool, real shadows.
An extra character the story didn't needRemoved from the whole video. Every character must earn its place.
Fixing one scene but not the others"Remove X" means from the whole video. Say it explicitly.
Rendering before lookingStills first, always. A 3-hour render of a mistake is 3 hours lost.
Losing hours to a cloud restartA resumable render that saves each frame and continues.
A number the sources disagreed onIf sources disagree, don't say the number.
Captions covering facesCaptions at 70% height; faces live at 35–60%.
Repeating the same correction every videoEvery correction becomes a line in CLAUDE.md.

The final checklist

Script

  • The first sentence shows the strangest moment, without explaining it.
  • Every fact is checked in two sources; disputed numbers left out.
  • There's a turn in the middle and a payoff near the end.
  • Everything planted early comes back.
  • It reads out loud within the target length.
  • Exactly one call to action.

Picture

  • 3–4 pictures in the first 5 seconds; the act is visible at 0 s.
  • Every set has a visible light source and real shadows.
  • The camera never stands still; whips have motion blur.
  • Nothing cut off, no captions over faces, hands touching what they hold.
  • Stills checked, then a half-size draft watched start to finish.

Sound

  • The same narrator voice and base director's notes as every video.
  • Music ~14 dB under the voice; effects sparse and soft.
  • -14 LUFS, true peak at or below -1 dB.

Posting

  • Title = the hook as a question. Cover = the hook frame + 2–4 words.
  • Posted at your usual time. Numbers checked after 48 hours.
  • A reason to follow: a series, a part 2, a daily promise.
